How to Design a Custom Electronics Enclosure That Survives Outdoors

By Aegis Engineered Solutions · October 20, 2026 · 4 min read

Outdoor electronics die from water, sun and condensation, not from circuitry. Plan entries, material, mounting and access first.

Custom electronics enclosures in Ontario fail outdoors for boring reasons, and every one of them is preventable at the design stage. Water finds the cable entry. Sun softens the plastic. Condensation pools where the board sits. None of these are circuit problems. They are box problems, and the box deserves the same planning as the electronics inside it. This guide covers entries, material, mounting and service access for a maker or homeowner putting electronics outside for the first time.

The audience is someone comfortable with basic tools and measurements. Anything involving mains or grid connected wiring belongs to a licensed electrician. Aegis designs and plans around licensed electrical work rather than doing it, and enclosures that touch mains equipment need that boundary stated in the plan.

Start with every cable entry

Water enters where cables enter, almost without exception. List every wire that must cross the enclosure wall: power in, sensor leads, antenna feed, status wiring. Each crossing gets a planned gland or grommet sized to the actual cable diameter, mounted on the bottom face or the lower third of a side wall, never on the top. Entries from below with drip loops in the cable cost almost nothing and defeat most rain.

Measure the real cables before choosing glands. A gland sized from memory either crushes the jacket or leaves a gap, and both fail within a season. Photograph the entry area with something familiar for scale and note what moves: cables to a gate sensor flex, cables to a fixed panel do not, and the entry must suit the movement.

Choose material for sun and cold

Ontario sun softens common printed plastics and chalks untreated boxes. If the enclosure sits in direct sun, choose a UV tolerant material or plan a sun shield that stands off from the box so air moves between them. Dark colours absorb heat that cooks batteries and shifts sensor readings, so prefer light colours for sun exposed boxes.

Cold brings its own trouble. Plastics that flex in summer crack in February, and fasteners loosen across freeze thaw cycles. Stainless or exterior rated fasteners only, with washers that spread the load on printed walls. An illustrative example with round numbers: a box with four lid screws on a ten centimetre span, torqued evenly in a cross pattern, holds its seal far longer than the same box with two screws cranked hard. The pattern matters more than the force.

Beat condensation and mount for service

Sealed boxes breathe with temperature swings, and breathing pulls in damp air that condenses on the coldest surface, usually the board. Leave a small drain at the lowest point rather than sealing the box absolutely. A drain the size of a pencil lead passes water out and almost no weather in. Keep the board raised off the floor of the box on standoffs so incidental moisture pools below the circuitry, not under it.

Mount the box where a person can reach it for maintenance. Soffits, posts and sheltered walls beat rooftops and fence ends that need a ladder in January. Note the reach in the plan: if servicing needs a ladder, say so, and say who brings it. A monitoring panel nobody checks becomes clutter, and an enclosure nobody opens becomes a mystery.

Prototype the fit, then hand over the notes

Custom builds include two physical iterations by default. Print or mock the box first and test the entries with the real cables before any electronics move in. Hose test the closed empty box from every side, open it, and look for the wet spots. Adjust once against reality rather than discovering the leak with a board inside.

The handoff includes the enclosure, the files behind it such as models or configuration notes, plain language notes on how it opens and what the indicators mean, and a support path. Record the gland sizes, the fastener types, the drain location and the maintenance routine, even if the routine is only a seasonal look at seals, cables and charge state.
