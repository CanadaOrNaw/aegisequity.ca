Planning solar powered motorized blinds starts with an honest power budget, not a shopping list. A blind motor sips energy in short bursts, which makes these projects look easy and causes most failures: a panel that covers summer use but starves in November, a battery baking in a hot window bay, a wire route nobody measured. This guide walks through battery, panel, wiring and enclosure planning in the order that prevents those surprises.

It is written for a technical homeowner comfortable with basic tools and measurements. Device level solar like this stays on the low voltage side of the wall. Anything involving mains or grid connected wiring is a job for a licensed electrician, and your plan should say so plainly where that boundary appears.

Budget the power in the weakest season

List what the blind motor draws per cycle and how many cycles a day it runs. Motors draw briefly and then sleep, so daily energy is small but spiky. Size the battery around the weakest season, not the best: a setup that breaks even in July can run dry in November when days are short and the blinds still move twice a day.

Give the battery a reserve of several dull days. Small sealed batteries suit this kind of load, and the enclosure section below covers where they live. Note the result as a plain requirement, for example a stated number of days of autonomy at the measured daily draw, so any later substitution can be checked against it.

Place the battery and charge controller first

Battery placement decides much of the rest. Batteries dislike heat, frost and damp in equal measure. A window bay collects all three across a Canadian year, so plan the battery for a shaded, ventilated, reachable spot nearby rather than inside the sunniest cavity. The charge controller sits beside it, kept dry and accessible for its indicator lights.

Measure the real space before choosing hardware. Photograph the location with something familiar for scale and note clearances, mounting surfaces and whether a person can reach the spot for maintenance. Every Aegis project starts from measured reality rather than a generic specification, and battery placement is where that habit pays off first.

Plan the panel placement and the wire route

The panel wants the best sun available, which is rarely the window the blind covers. South facing roof surface, a railing or a nearby wall can all work. Walk the route the wire must travel from the panel to the controller and on to the blind motor, and measure it. Roof panel routes cross flashing, eaves and gutters, and each crossing needs a planned seal and fastener, not a bead of sealant discovered later.

Keep the route short, protected from foot traffic and lawn equipment, and separated from anything that moves. Write down every penetration and how it will be sealed. If the route must cross mains wiring or enter the main panel, that portion belongs to a licensed electrician. Aegis designs and plans around licensed electrical work rather than doing it.

Enclosure and install plan

The enclosure is the part the weather tests. Controllers and connections need a weather ready box with a lid that closes over drip loops, cable entries from below, and room to work with gloved hands. Stainless or exterior rated fasteners only. Label every wire at both ends before the lid closes.

An illustrative example with round numbers shows how the pieces combine. Suppose a single blind runs four cycles a day at a small measured draw, needing roughly ten units of energy daily. A battery holding fifty units gives about five dull days of reserve. A panel rated near twenty units per average sun hour replaces the daily use in under an hour of good light, leaving margin for weak months. The install plan lists the roof mount, a measured ten pace wire route with two sealed penetrations, the controller beside the battery in a shaded utility space, and the motor connection as the final step. Round numbers only; your measurements replace every one of them.

Prototype small and plan the handoff

Custom builds include two physical iterations by default. Prove the chain on a bench first: panel, controller, battery and motor on a table, shaded and unshaded, before anything is fastened to the house. Then install once, watch it through a dull stretch, and adjust.

A finished build hands over the thing, the files and notes behind it, and a support path. Keep a one page record: panel position and tilt, battery type and replacement interval, wire route sketch, controller settings and what the indicator lights mean. Name who checks it and when, even if the routine is only a seasonal look at seals, cables and charge state.
