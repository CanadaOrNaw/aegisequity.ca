Custom 3D printed parts in Durham Region usually start with something small and annoying: a broken clip that the manufacturer no longer sells, a mount that doesn't exist for your exact camera, or an adapter between two pieces of equipment that were never designed to meet. The printing is the easy part. The hard part is making something that fits the real object in front of you, not a drawing of what it should be.

This note walks through what actually decides fit, and what to gather before you ask anyone, Aegis included, to model a part.

1. Measure the real object, not the spec sheet

Published dimensions describe the ideal part. The thing on your wall has paint on it, a bit of wear, and whatever tolerance its factory worked to. A mount modelled from a spec sheet can be off by just enough to rattle or refuse to seat.

  • Use calipers where you can. A tape measure is fine for overall size, but holes, slots, tab thicknesses and wall gaps need calipers.
  • Measure the mating parts, too. A clip has to fit the panel it grips, and a bracket has to fit both the device and the surface it bolts to.
  • Measure more than once. Take the same dimension in two or three places. If a slot measures differently at each end, that matters.
  • Note what moves. Hinges, latches and anything that has to flex or rotate change what the part has to allow for.

2. Take photos that show how it connects

Good photos save a round of guessing. Shoot the broken or missing part from several angles, then the place it goes. Put a ruler or a coin in the frame for scale. Most useful of all is a photo of how it attaches: the screws, the tabs, the lip it hooks under.

If you still have the broken original, keep every piece. Even a snapped part shows wall thickness, hole positions and where it failed, and where it failed tells you where the new one needs more material.

3. Choose the material for where the part lives

Material choice changes more than strength. It changes how a part behaves over time, and that affects fit.

  • PLA is easy to print accurately, but it softens at fairly low temperatures. A PLA part on a dashboard, near a heater or in summer sun can slowly deform.
  • PETG is tougher, a little more flexible, and handles more heat than PLA. It's a common choice for functional brackets and clips.
  • ASA holds up better outdoors, where sunlight breaks down many other plastics over time.
  • TPU is flexible, useful for bumpers, grips, seals and anything that has to squeeze into place.

So say where the part will live: indoors or outside, near heat, in a vehicle, under load, or snapping on and off every day. That decides the material, and the material decides how tight the fit can be.

4. Expect the second print to be the keeper

Printed parts shrink a little as they cool. Holes tend to come out slightly small, and pegs that have to press into holes need a deliberate clearance. Different materials and print orientations behave differently. You can model for all of that, but the fastest route to a part that really fits is usually to print it, test it on the real object, adjust the model, and print again.

That's why Aegis plans two physical iterations by default on custom builds. The first print answers the questions a measurement can't. The second one is the part you install. A simple part may be right the first time, and a complex one may need a third pass. Planning for iteration up front is cheaper than pretending it won't happen.

5. Remember that print direction affects strength

A 3D print is built in layers, and it's weakest between them. A thin clip printed standing up can snap cleanly along a layer line the first time it flexes, while the same clip printed flat can take the load. When a part has to bend, hook or carry weight, its orientation on the printer is part of the design, not an afterthought.

What to send when you ask for a custom part

Whoever makes the part, this list gets you to a good fit faster:

  • Photos of the part (or the gap where it goes), with something in frame for scale
  • Your measurements, and which ones you're unsure of
  • The make and model of the device or equipment, if there is one
  • Where the part lives: indoors, outdoors, heat, vibration, load
  • What you've already tried
  • What success looks like: holds a camera steady, survives a winter outside, snaps on and off daily
